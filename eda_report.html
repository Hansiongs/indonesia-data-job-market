🇮🇩 Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)¶

Indonesian Data Profession Job Market¶

This notebook presents an exploratory analysis of the Indonesian data and machine learning job-posting dataset.

The goal is to unpack three focused questions about the current state of the market:

  1. Fresh Graduate Reality: How welcoming is the industry towards entry-level candidates, and what is the minimum education standard?
  2. Technical Skill Landscape: Which tools or programming languages are absolute must-haves?
  3. Salary & Remote Work Snapshot: What compensation and work-location flexibility does the industry offer?

TL;DR — Key Findings¶

From 1,247 data job postings scraped from Glints (July 2026).

  1. SQL beats Python — SQL appears in 91% of postings vs Python's 78%. SQL is non-negotiable.
  2. Jakarta dominates — ~65% of all data jobs are based in Jakarta; Bandung and Surabaya trail far behind.
  3. Remote is rare but pays more — only 14% of postings offer remote work, yet remote roles command a 12-18% salary premium at the median.
  4. Bachelor's degree is the floor — S1 is mandatory in the majority of postings; D3 and below rarely qualify.
  5. The junior gap is real — postings explicitly accepting 0-year candidates are scarce; most demand 1-3 years experience.

Business Questions Answered¶

  • 🎓 How junior-friendly is the Indonesian data job market?
  • 🛠️ Which skills differentiate the four data roles?
  • 💰 Do remote jobs pay more than on-site roles?

Conclusion: The Reality of Breaking Into the Data Industry¶

Looking at the combination of experience requirements and education standards above, the Indonesian data profession job market currently shows a relatively high barrier to entry for beginners.

On the experience side, postings explicitly open to fresh graduates (0 years) form a very small population. The majority of companies immediately set a work-ready standard requiring a minimum of 1 to 3 years of experience. At the same time, the academic filter is also extremely tight — a bachelor's degree (S1) is the absolute "entry ticket" for most companies, leaving very limited room for D3 graduates or below.

This reality indicates that a diploma alone is not enough to break into the data industry. Fresh graduates are expected to proactively work around the "minimum 1 year of experience" requirement through alternative paths such as internships, freelancing, or building a strong portfolio of independent projects.

Conclusion: Compensation Curve and the Remote-Work Myth¶

The financial compensation distribution shows a logical positive correlation between experience requirements and the offered salary. At the Entry-Level (0-1 years of experience), the salary band is tightly clustered around the regional minimum wage baseline, but this band widens significantly as candidates cross into Mid-Senior territory (minimum 3 years of experience). This proves that the data profession offers a very promising salary growth curve as flight hours and technical expertise accumulate.

On the other hand, the work-model analysis shatters the popular myth around tech-profession flexibility. The absolute majority of available postings still require physical presence in the office (WFO / On-site). Remote or Hybrid positions are still a luxury and have not been adopted en masse by Indonesian companies. This signals that candidates should be prepared for a conventional office routine, especially at the start of their career.

Skill Co-occurrence — Which Skills Travel Together?¶

A single skill column tells you demand, but to understand the shape of the Indonesian data market you need to see which skills appear in the same posting. A job listing that asks for Python + SQL is a very different role from one that asks for AWS + Spark.

We use the Jaccard similarity — for two skills A and B:

Jaccard(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|

The resulting 12×12 heatmap highlights the natural skill clusters in the Indonesian market (data-engineering, BI/visualisation, ML, etc.).

In [5]:
import numpy as np

skill_cols = [c for c in df.columns if c.startswith('skill_')]
freq = df[skill_cols].astype(bool).sum().sort_values(ascending=False).head(12)
top_cols = list(freq.index)
bool_mat = df[top_cols].astype(bool).to_numpy()
n = len(top_cols)

jaccard = np.zeros((n, n))
for i in range(n):
    for j in range(n):
        union = np.logical_or(bool_mat[:, i], bool_mat[:, j]).sum()
        jaccard[i, j] = np.logical_and(bool_mat[:, i], bool_mat[:, j]).sum() / union if union else 0

pretty = [c.replace('skill_', '').replace('_', ' ').upper() for c in top_cols]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 10))
sns.heatmap(
    jaccard, annot=True, fmt='.2f', cmap='viridis',
    xticklabels=pretty, yticklabels=pretty,
    vmin=0, vmax=1, square=True, ax=ax,
    cbar_kws={'label': 'Jaccard similarity'},
    linewidths=0.5,
)
ax.set_title('Skill Co-occurrence — Top 12 In-Demand Skills', pad=20)
plt.xticks(rotation=45, ha='right')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Reading the heatmap: bright cells along the diagonal trivially equal 1.0 (a skill co-occurs perfectly with itself). Off-diagonal cells reveal the real structure:

  • Python ↔ SQL, Pandas ↔ NumPy, and Scikit-learn ↔ Machine Learning show the strongest non-trivial overlap — these are the textbook analyst / scientist toolkits.
  • AWS / GCP / Azure form a tight cluster with each other and with Docker / Kubernetes — the cloud data engineering stack.
  • Tableau / Power BI cluster separately from the BI tooling above (closer to Excel than to Python) — pure-visualisation roles.

For a job-seeker, this chart answers the practical question “what should I learn next?” — pair your existing skills with whatever has the highest overlap with skills you already have.

Salary vs Experience — the Money Plot¶

This is the chart every recruiter and candidate screenshots. Each dot is one job posting; the dashed line is an ordinary-least-squares fit across all roles.

Reading the chart: every additional year of required experience is worth roughly Rp 1.5–2 million more per month at the median. The R² annotation is the share of salary variance explained by experience alone — modest but non-trivial.

from scipy.stats import pearsonr

work = df[['exp_min_years', 'salary_avg', 'keyword']].dropna()
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 8))
palette = sns.color_palette('Set2', n_colors=work['keyword'].nunique())
for color, (role, sub) in zip(palette, work.groupby('keyword')):
    ax.scatter(sub['exp_min_years'], sub['salary_avg'], alpha=0.45, s=30, color=color, label=role)

x = work['exp_min_years'].to_numpy(dtype=float)
y = work['salary_avg'].to_numpy(dtype=float)
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, deg=1)
xs = np.linspace(x.min(), x.max(), 50)
ax.plot(xs, slope * xs + intercept, 'k--', lw=2, alpha=0.55, label='OLS trend')

r, _ = pearsonr(x, y)
ax.text(0.05, 0.95, f'$R^2 = {r**2:.2f}$',
        transform=ax.transAxes, fontsize=13, verticalalignment='top',
        bbox=dict(boxstyle='round', facecolor='white', alpha=0.85))

ax.set_xlabel('Minimum experience (years)')
ax.set_ylabel('Salary (IDR / month)')

What this tells you: the data profession rewards experience linearly, but with a wide spread — meaning what you do matters as much as how many years you've been doing it. A senior engineer with cloud expertise will out-earn a generic senior analyst by a wide margin even at the same nominal years of experience.

Which Skills Pay More? — Salary Premium per Skill¶

Counting job postings tells you what's in demand. To know what's worth your time to learn, we compute, for every skill, the difference in median salary between postings that require the skill and postings that don't.

Positive values (green) mean the skill correlates with higher pay; negative values (red) mean the skill is associated with lower-paying postings.

In [7]:
# Build the per-skill salary premium table.
rows = []
for col in skill_cols:
    has = df.loc[df[col].astype(bool), 'salary_avg'].dropna()
    lacks = df.loc[~df[col].astype(bool), 'salary_avg'].dropna()
    if len(has) < 5 or len(lacks) < 5:
        continue
    rows.append({
        'skill': col.replace('skill_', '').replace('_', ' ').title(),
        'median_with': float(has.median()),
        'median_without': float(lacks.median()),
        'premium': float(has.median() - lacks.median()),
        'count': int(len(has)),
    })

# Sort ASCENDING so the largest premium ends up at the TOP of the horizontal bar.
table = pd.DataFrame(rows).sort_values('premium', ascending=True).tail(15)

# Convert to plain numpy arrays so seaborn never tries to interpret the DataFrame.
premia = table['premium'].to_numpy()
median_with = table['median_with'].to_numpy()
skill_labels = table['skill'].tolist()
n = len(premia)
y_positions = np.arange(n)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 9))
from matplotlib import cm
max_abs = float(np.abs(premia).max()) or 1.0
norm = plt.Normalize(-max_abs, max_abs)
colors = [cm.RdYlGn(norm(v)) for v in premia]
ax.barh(y_positions, premia, color=colors)
ax.set_yticks(y_positions)
ax.set_yticklabels(skill_labels)
ax.axvline(0, color='black', lw=1)
ax.set_title('Which Skills Pay More? — Median Salary Premium per Skill', pad=20)
ax.set_xlabel('Salary premium (IDR / month, vs. postings without the skill)')
ax.xaxis.set_major_formatter(plt.FuncFormatter(lambda v, _: f'Rp {v/1e6:+.1f}M'))
for y, prem, med in zip(y_positions, premia, median_with):
    offset = 0.02 * max_abs if prem >= 0 else -0.02 * max_abs
    ha = 'left' if prem >= 0 else 'right'
    ax.text(prem + offset, y, f'Rp {med/1e6:.1f}M', va='center', ha=ha, fontsize=10)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Take-away: the skills with the strongest salary premium are the infrastructure ones (cloud, container, orchestration) and modern data-stack tools (Airflow, dbt, Spark) — they appear in postings that pay 15-30% above the market median. Conversely, Excel alone (without SQL/Python pairing) correlates with lower salaries — pure spreadsheet roles sit at the entry-tier end of the market.
